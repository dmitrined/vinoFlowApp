Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) is perhaps the most debated and, at the same time, indispensable additive in classical winemaking. Its role is multifaceted: from blocking undesirable microflora to preventing the oxidation of aromatic compounds. In this article, we will examine the physicochemical foundations of sulfitation and the mathematics behind the calculations in VinoFlow.

## The Many Faces of SO₂: Chemical Equilibrium

When added to wine, sulfur dioxide does not remain in a single form. It enters a complex dynamic equilibrium, splitting into three main fractions:

1.  **Bound SO₂**: Combines with sugars, aldehydes (especially acetaldehyde), and ketones. This part is practically inert.
2.  **Free SO₂**: The fraction not bound to other components. It is this part that provides protection for the wine.
3.  **Molecular SO₂**: A small part of the free sulfur dioxide gas that possesses maximum antiseptic power.

A professional oenologist knows that the effectiveness of sulfitation depends directly on the **pH of the wine**. The lower the pH, the higher the proportion of active molecular SO₂, and the smaller the dose required for stabilization.

## Forms of Addition and Dosage Mathematics

Depending on the technological task, winemakers use various preparations. Each requires a specific approach to calculation.

### 1. Potassium Pyrosulfite (Metabisulfite)
A powdered form, most popular in small and medium-sized wineries. Upon addition, it releases about **50%** pure sulfur dioxide.

> **M (g) = (Volume (l) × ΔSO₂ (mg/l)) / (10 × Concentration (%))**

### 2. Liquid Solutions (Sulfurous Acid)
Usually represent a 5–15% aqueous solution of the gas. Convenient for automated addition.

> **V (ml) = (Volume (l) × ΔSO₂ (mg/l)) / Concentration (g/l)**

<Note type="info">
When calculating using liquid forms, the concentration is often specified in grams per liter. For example, a 5% solution corresponds to 50 g/l of pure SO₂.
</Note>
